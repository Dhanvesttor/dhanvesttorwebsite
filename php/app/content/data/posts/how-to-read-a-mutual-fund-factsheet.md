A factsheet is a fund's monthly report card. It is two pages long, published by every fund house, and free. Here is how to read one without a finance degree.

## Start with what the fund is for

The first paragraph states the investment objective and the category: large cap, flexi cap, corporate bond, and so on. If the objective does not match the goal you bought it for, stop here.

## Six numbers that matter

**Expense ratio.** What you pay each year, taken from the fund's assets before returns are reported. Regular plans include distributor commission; direct plans do not. Lower is better, all else equal, but all else is rarely equal.

**Top ten holdings.** What percentage of the fund sits in its ten largest positions. Forty percent is concentrated; twenty-five is diversified. Neither is wrong, but you should know which you own.

**Sector weights.** Compare the fund's sector allocation with its benchmark. A fund that is heavily overweight one sector is making a bet. Decide whether you want to be in that bet.

**Portfolio turnover.** How much of the portfolio changed in a year. One hundred percent means, on average, the whole portfolio was replaced. High turnover means higher costs and, for you, less predictability.

**Benchmark.** The index the fund measures itself against. Returns only mean something next to it. A 14% year is excellent against a 9% benchmark and poor against a 20% one.

**Rolling returns**, if shown. Point-to-point returns depend on the start date. Rolling returns show how the fund did over every three or five year window, which is closer to what you will experience.

## Three numbers that distract

**One-year return.** Too short to tell you anything about skill.

**Star ratings.** Backward-looking and category-relative. Useful as a filter, useless as a decision.

**AUM alone.** A large fund is not a safe fund, and a small fund is not a nimble one. Size matters only at the extremes.
