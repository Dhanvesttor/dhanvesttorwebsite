A bear market is usually defined as a fall of 20% or more from a recent high. The definition is tidy. The experience is not.

## How it unfolds

Falls rarely happen in one move. A typical bear market has three phases. First, a sharp drop that feels like an overreaction and attracts buyers. Second, a long grind in which rallies fail and the news gets worse. Third, capitulation, when even patient investors sell, volumes spike, and nobody wants to talk about equities at dinner.

The third phase is where the best long-term returns are made. It is also when it feels most wrong to invest.

## Why it feels worse than the numbers

A 20% fall in a portfolio you watched grow for three years erases a visible number. Research on loss aversion suggests we feel losses roughly twice as strongly as equivalent gains. Add daily headlines and a phone that shows you the red figure every morning, and a sensible plan starts to feel reckless.

## Four things to do

**Check the goal, not the price.** If the money is for a goal more than five years away, the fall has changed nothing about your plan. If the goal is near, the question is why equity-heavy money was still sitting there, and the fix is allocation, not panic.

**Keep the SIP running.** The same monthly amount now buys more units. This is the one time rupee-cost averaging earns its name.

**Re-read what you own.** For PMS clients, our monthly statement shows every holding. For fund investors, the factsheet shows the top holdings. Ask whether the businesses have changed, or only the prices.

**Decide in advance.** Write down now, in a calm market, what you will do when the next fall comes. Then do that.

## Three things not to do

Do not check the portfolio daily. Do not sell to "wait for clarity"; clarity arrives after the recovery. Do not switch strategies in the middle of a drawdown because another one did better last quarter.
